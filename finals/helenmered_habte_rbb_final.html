Introduction

Family planning plays a critical role in improving maternal and child health, empowering women, and supporting economic development. In Ethiopia, the promotion of contraceptive use has been a major component of national health policy over the past two decades. Despite improvements in awareness and access, challenges remain in equitable access and consistent uptake of modern contraceptive methods across regions and socio-demographic groups.

This report analyzes data from the Ethiopia Demographic and Health Survey (DHS) 2011 to examine patterns of contraceptive use among women of reproductive age (15-49). The goal is to better understand how usage varies by education level, wealth status, place of residence, and age group. By identifying trends and disparities, this analysis aims to inform future strategies for more inclusive family planning programs.

Objectives

  • To assess women’s knowledge and use of family planning methods in Ethiopia

  • To explore how family planning use and access vary by education, age, wealth, and place of residence

Methods of contraceptive use

The overall distribution of contraceptive method use among Ethiopian women aged 15–49, based on the 2011 DHS data, shows that 76.2% of women reported not using any contraceptive method. Among those who did, modern methods were the most commonly used (23.2%), followed by traditional methods (0.6%) and a negligible share using folkloric methods (0.0%).These results highlight a significant unmet need for contraception, as the vast majority of women remain outside the formal family planning system. Despite the presence of modern methods, overall uptake remains low relative to the target population.

Contraceptive Use by Socio-Demographic Factors

Education

Women with higher levels of education are more likely to use modern contraceptive methods. Those with no education predominantly do not use any method, while women with secondary and higher education show the most diverse method adoption, especially favoring modern options. This highlights the critical role of education in enabling informed reproductive decisions and increasing acceptance of modern contraception.

Age Group

Contraceptive use is highest among women aged 25–34, aligning with peak reproductive years. The youngest (15–19) and oldest (45–49) groups show the highest rates of non-use, suggesting a combination of lower demand and limited service access or autonomy. The low uptake in younger women may also reflect cultural or societal barriers around youth contraceptive access.

Wealth Quantile

Use of modern methods increases consistently with wealth. Women in the poorest quintiles rely heavily on no method at all, whereas modern contraceptive uptake is most prevalent among the richest, indicating affordability and access as potential barriers. This trend emphasizes the need for cost-effective, publicly available contraceptive services for lower-income populations.

Family planning and sources of contraceptives by residence

Both access to contraceptives and knowledge of family planning vary by place of residence. Urban women predominantly obtain contraceptives from private sources, while rural women rely more on public facilities such as health posts and centers. This highlights the urban advantage in healthcare options, while rural areas depend on government infrastructure. Despite these access differences, awareness of family planning is high in both groups, with slightly greater knowledge reported among urban women. This suggests that the primary barriers are not informational, but structural — including service availability, affordability, and logistical access.

Conclusion

This analysis reveals significant disparities in contraceptive access and usage among Ethiopian women. Modern methods are more commonly used than traditional ones, yet over three-quarters of women reported not using any method. Socio-demographic factors particularly education, wealth, and age strongly influence contraceptive behavior. Uptake is highest among wealthier, educated women aged 25–34. Additionally, rural women rely more on public sources, while urban women benefit from a wider mix including private providers. Although awareness of family planning is broadly high, access remains unequal, underlining the need for targeted public health interventions.
