INTRODUCTION

Background: Cholera as a Public Health Threat in Ethiopia

Cholera continues to pose a major public health threat in Ethiopia, especially in communities with inadequate access to clean water and proper sanitation facilities. As a waterborne disease, cholera flourishes in settings where water infrastructure is insufficient, sanitation systems are lacking, and hygiene practices are suboptimal. Limited access to safe drinking water and poor sanitation not only increase the risk of transmission but also hinder effective disease control. These challenges are often compounded by factors such as overcrowding, seasonal flooding, and population displacement, turning cholera outbreaks into urgent health and humanitarian crises.

Despite global progress in cholera control, Ethiopia continues to experience recurrent outbreaks, with disproportionately high burdens in rural and low-income households. Understanding the multidimensional risk factors, ranging from environmental conditions to socioeconomic disparities, is critical for designing effective, targeted interventions.

Project Overview: Analyzing Cholera Risk Using DHS Data

This project examines the key drivers of cholera vulnerability using the 2019 Ethiopia Demographic and Health Survey (DHS), a nationally representative dataset that includes water and sanitation indicators as well as sociodemographic factors. By leveraging this comprehensive dataset, the project aims to:

  1. Assess cholera risk through composite indicators: Constructing a Cholera Risk Score by integrating variables related to water access and sanitation.
  2. Identify high-risk populations: Disaggregating risk by geographic region, urban/rural residence, and wealth quintiles to uncover disparities.
  3. Support evidence-based interventions: Highlighting hotspots and structural inequities to guide public health planning.

By leveraging routine survey data like the DHS, this analysis moves beyond reactive outbreak response, supporting proactive, equity-focused public health strategies. The findings aim to contribute to Ethiopia’s broader cholera elimination efforts and the global roadmap for reducing cholera deaths by 90% by 2030.

METHODOLOGY AND APPROACH

This analysis used data from the 2019 Ethiopia Demographic and Health Survey (DHS) to explore multidimensional cholera risk factors. Key variables included water sources, sanitation facilities, household wealth index, urban-rural residence, education level, and age of women. These variables were selected based on their relevance to cholera vulnerability and aligned with established WASH and socio-demographic indicators. Data were cleaned, recoded, and analyzed using descriptive statistics and visualization tools. A composite Cholera Risk Score was developed to quantify household-level vulnerability, incorporating unsafe water and poor sanitation indicators. Risk scores were then compared across regions, residence types, education levels, wealth quintiles, and age groups to identify patterns and high-risk populations. The analysis aimed to move beyond reactive outbreak responses by using routine survey data to guide proactive, equity-focused public health interventions.

Distribution of Water and Sanitation Access Among Households

Most households (37.9%) use pit latrines without a slab or open pits, and 31% have no toilet facility at all. Only a small proportion use improved sanitation, such as flush toilets (8.7%) or VIP latrines (2.6%). Regarding water sources, nearly 30% rely on public taps or standpipes, while 13.3% use surface water like rivers or ponds. Access to piped water is limited, with just 3.3% having it in their dwelling and 15.2% in their yard. Use of bottled or protected water sources remains relatively low, highlighting major gaps in access to safe water and sanitation.

Education Level Predicts Cholera Risk: Higher Education Associated with Lower Vulnerability

Individuals with no education face the highest mean risk score (1.23), while those with higher education have the lowest (0.07). Secondary and primary education groups show intermediate risks (1.02 and 0.38, respectively). This gradient suggests that education may mitigate cholera risk—possibly through improved hygiene awareness, healthcare access, or socioeconomic advantages.

Among the poorest, 55% are in the high-risk category, while only 5% of the richest fall into this group. Overall, wealthier households are significantly less vulnerable to cholera, likely due to better access to clean water, sanitation, and healthcare services.

CONCLUSION

This analysis demonstrates that cholera risk in Ethiopia is strongly associated with poor access to safe water and sanitation, low education levels, rural residence, and lower wealth quintiles. Regions such as Afar, Somali, and SNNPR exhibit consistently higher risk scores, indicating the need for targeted WASH (Water, Sanitation, and Hygiene) interventions. The findings also reveal that education and wealth can serve as protective factors, likely by improving awareness and access to hygiene resources. By leveraging DHS data and composite risk scoring, this project highlights vulnerable populations and geographic hotspots—providing valuable insights to guide public health planning and cholera prevention efforts in Ethiopia.
