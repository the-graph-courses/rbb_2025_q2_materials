INTRODUCTION

1. Background

Efforts to reduce maternal, neonatal, and child mortality—including nutrition (RMNCAH+N)—have yielded notable results between 2011 and 2018. For instance, the Maternal Mortality Ratio (MMR) decreased from 782 to 476 deaths per 100,000 live births, while modern contraceptive prevalence increased, and the unmet need for family planning fell to 23.5%. Additionally, the under-five mortality rate declined from 122 to 80 deaths per 1,000 live births during the same period (EDS-MICS 2011, DHS 2018). These encouraging results, however, conceal regional disparities. The Adamaoua, East, Far North, and North regions continue to exhibit the most pronounced inequalities both in the supply of and demand for RMNCAH+N services. To achieve these results, several strategies were implemented by the Government and its Development Partners. In particular, the leading complications responsible for maternal deaths.

According to the World Health Organization (WHO) (1), the leading complications responsible for nearly 75% of maternal deaths include:

  • Severe hemorrhage (primarily postpartum);

  • Infections (typically occurring after childbirth);

  • Hypertensive disorders during pregnancy (such as preeclampsia and eclampsia);

  • Complications during labor and delivery;

  • Unsafe abortion.

According to the World Health Organization (WHO), one of the key interventions to address the leading causes of maternal mortality is the monitoring of pregnant women through a continuum of care throughout pregnancy—namely, antenatal care (ANC). WHO recommends that women attend at least eight ANC contacts during pregnancy, with the first occurring before the 16th week of gestation.

This naturally raises the question: what is the impact of these interventions on pregnancy outcomes? Does the specific context of Cameroon support the existence of this relationship—and if so, with what degree of intensity?

2. Objectives

The main objective is to assess the contribution of antenatal care to the reduction of maternal mortality. Specifically, the study aims to:

  1. Analyze of the structure of the population of pregnant women;

  2. Analyze the demand antenatal care services;

  3. Analyse of antenatal care disparities

TOOLS AND METHODOLOGY

1. Tools

The tools to be used for the implementation of this study include Microsoft Office Suite. The R software will be employed for data processing and analysis, including descriptive, associative, and comparative analyses, among others.

2. Methodology

This article will use data from the 2018 Cameroon Demographic and Health Survey (DHS 2018). The primary objective of this survey was to collect and analyze data on reproductive health, maternal, newborn, child and adolescent health, as well as family well-being. The target population for this study consists of women of reproductive age (15–49 years) who use maternal health services—specifically, women who had at least one live birth in the five years preceding the survey.

The indicators calculated as part of this work will be slightly different from those in a similar study because the data has not been extrapolated.

A descriptive analysis made it possible to understand how women of reproductive age are distributed according to their geographical location, the level of household well-being to which they belong, their level of education, and finally, their religious affiliation. A descriptive analysis also helped identify the determinants of the use of prenatal consultation services.

For the analysis of disparities, we constructed a specific indicator: the proportion of women of reproductive age who, during their last pregnancy, had at least four prenatal consultations or (inclusively) attended at least four prenatal consultations during their pregnancy. Thus, for the analysis of disparities, we compared this statistic based on inequality variables (statistical regions, level of education, place of residence, level of well-being).

Finally, it should be noted that the results obtained in this study have not been extrapolated to the total population.

RESULTS

1. Description of basic demographics

The objective of this section is to assess the structure of the population of women of reproductive age. This presentation is organized into three main areas: first, the age structure by region; then, the structure of women of reproductive age according to household well-being; followed by the structure based on level of education; and finally, the structure by religious affiliation.

  • Structure of age and region

Graph 1 shows the distribution of women of reproductive age according to age and region of origin. The average age of surveyed women of reproductive age is approximately 28. This average age is generally consistent across the twelve survey regions, although some regional disparities are observed

The South-West region has the lowest age dispersion among women of reproductive age, while the South and West regions exhibit the highest levels of age dispersion within this population group.

  • Structure of wealth and region

Graph 2 shows the distribution of women of working age by region and according to the well-being of the households to which they belong. The cities of Yaoundé and Douala have a relatively low proportion of poor women compared to other surveyed regions. In contrast, the regions of Adamawa, East, Far North, and North have a higher concentration of women from very poor and poor households. These regions are also considered priority zones for Reproductive, Maternal, Newborn, Child and Adolescent Health, including Nutrition (RMNCAH+N).

The cities of Yaounde and Douala each constitute a distinct survey region due to their unique administrative, political, and economic structures.

  • Structure of Education and region

Graph 3 shows that women of reproductive age interviewed for this survey with a primary level of education or less are predominantly found in priority regions compared to other areas. Additionally, women of reproductive age with a secondary level of education or higher are mainly concentrated in the cities of Yaounde and Douala.

The other regions have a more or less balanced distribution of women of reproductive age with primary and secondary levels of education.

  • Structure of religion and region

Cameroon is a secular country, which allows for the coexistence of all religions. Nevertheless, it is evident that certain religions are more firmly established in specific regions. Graph 4 illustrates that in the northern regions, Islam is the predominant religion, whereas in other regions, Catholicism holds the majority.

3. Analysis of antenatal care disparities

This section analyses disparities in the use of prenatal consultation services. This disparity analysis is conducted based on place of residence, region of origin, poverty level, and educational level of women of childbearing age. The dependent variable is the proportion of women of childbearing age who had at least four prenatal visits during their pregnancy or who had their first prenatal consultation before the end of the third month.

Graph 8 clearly illustrates the existence of disparities based on the disaggregation variables. Considering the survey region, disparities are observed between regions. The best-represented regions are the cities of Yaoundé and Douala. In contrast, the regions of Adamawa, East, Far North, and North are the lowest ranked. They are followed by the Northwest and Southwest regions.

DISCUSSION

The disparity analysis implemented has revealed a range of realities, both geographically and individually. This situation is not surprising, as it confirms findings from previous studies on the subject. Earlier Cameroon Household Surveys have shown that the poorest regions in Cameroon are Adamawa, East, Far North, and North. Given the current crisis, the Northwest and Southwest regions are also beginning to face significant problems, resulting in the deterioration of SRMNEA+N (Reproductive, Maternal, Newborn, Child, and Adolescent Health and Nutrition) indicators.

Several initiatives have been taken to designate these areas as SRMNEA priority regions in Cameroon. Although the results are slow to emerge, efforts are ongoing.

For the other disaggregation variables, it’s easy to understand that a woman who is not sufficiently educated cannot sustainable support herself. She tends to depend entirely on her partner, who may not always have a high level of education. Additionally, when accounting for factors related to religion and customs, it becomes clear why pregnant women with less education are less likely to use reproductive health services than those with a higher level of education.

Well-being and place of residence also play important roles in the use of prenatal consultation (ANC) services. An analysis of the health system shows that health services are more adequate in urban areas than in rural ones. Thus, it is understandable why service utilization is lower in rural areas. Even when a woman is willing to go to a hospital, access to services is not always guaranteed. Moreover, healthcare services come at a cost. One needs financial resources to access a health facility for consultation.

To address these barriers, several programs—such as the “Health Voucher” initiative—have been implemented. The aim of this project was to improve demand for reproductive health services by offering vouchers to women at very affordable prices.

CONCLUSION

The analysis carried out in this study provided an opportunity to revisit the use of antenatal care (ANC) services and the disparities associated with their utilization. For this purpose, data from the 2018 Demographic and Health Survey (DHS) were used. The analysis focused on women of childbearing age who had a live birth within the five years preceding the survey.

The results revealed that disparities exist in the utilization of ANC services.

These findings should prompt the Government to take action and support the implementation of innovative strategies to address and reduce these disparities.
